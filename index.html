先史・古代

Prehistoric & Ancient Japan

c. 14,000 BCE – 710 CE
Historical Event
c. 14,000 – 300 BCE
Jōmon Period — World's Oldest Pottery Culture
Hunter-gatherers produce the world's oldest known pottery — cord-marked Jōmon-ware — establishing a remarkably stable culture lasting over 10,000 years across the archipelago. Complex villages, pit dwellings, and elaborate burial practices develop without agriculture.
PrehistoricPottery
Religion
c. 14,000 – 300 BCE
Jōmon Animism & Shamanism
The earliest spiritual practice — all things possess spirit (tama). Shamans communed with nature spirits through trances and rituals. Dogū clay figurines with exaggerated features suggest fertility cults and healing practices tied to life-death cycles.
AnimismShamanismDogū
Historical Event
c. 300 BCE – 300 CE
Yayoi Revolution — Rice Agriculture Transforms Japan
Wet-rice cultivation arrives from continental Asia, triggering population growth, social stratification, and the first organized warfare. Bronze bells (dōtaku) and iron tools transform material culture. Distinct political entities — including Yamatai under the shamanic queen Himiko — emerge.
AgricultureHimiko
Foreign Contact
c. 300 BCE – 538 CE
China & Korea — First Contact & Cultural Transmission
Chinese histories (Wei Zhi) record Himiko's envoys to the Cao Wei court in 238 CE. Korean kingdoms — Baekje, Goguryeo, Silla — transmit Chinese writing (kanji), Confucian ethics, Buddhist sutras, bronze-casting, iron-working, and silk-weaving to Japan. The scholar Wani brings the Analects of Confucius.
🇨🇳 China & 🇰🇷 Korean Peninsula
DiplomacyWritingBuddhism
Religion
c. 300 BCE – 538 CE
Proto-Shinto — Kami Worship Crystallizes
Animism evolves into structured veneration of kami — sacred spirits of nature, ancestors, and creation forces. Agricultural rites, ritual purification (harae), sacred groves (himorogi), and shrine spaces define emerging practice. No scriptures, no founder — Shinto grows organically from the land.
KamiShinto Origins
Mythology
Age of Gods
Kuniumi — The Birth of the Islands
The divine couple Izanagi and Izanami stir the primordial ocean with a jeweled spear. Drops form Onogoroshima, the first island. They birth the Japanese archipelago and kami of wind, sea, and fire. When Izanami dies giving birth to the fire god, Izanagi descends to Yomi (the underworld) — origin of death and pollution.
CreationIzanagiIzanami
Mythology
Age of Gods
Amaterasu, the Imperial Regalia & Divine Descent
The sun goddess Amaterasu retreats into a cave, plunging the world into darkness. The kami lure her out with a sacred mirror and Ame-no-Uzume's ecstatic dance — origin of kagura. Susanoo slays Yamata no Orochi, discovering the sword Kusanagi. Amaterasu sends her grandson Ninigi to earth with the mirror, sword, and jewel. His descendant Jimmu becomes the first Emperor.
AmaterasuSusanooImperial Line
Historical Event
c. 250 – 538 CE
Kofun Period — Rise of the Yamato Imperial State
Massive keyhole-shaped burial mounds (kofun) signal the rise of the Yamato clan. The largest — Emperor Nintoku's tomb — exceeds the Great Pyramid in area. Haniwa clay figures guard the dead. The Yamato court consolidates control over much of Honshu, forging Japan's first proto-state and claiming divine descent from Amaterasu.
YamatoKofun Tombs
Historical Event
538 – 710 CE
Buddhism's Arrival & the Taika Reforms
Buddhism officially enters from Baekje (538/552), sparking the Soga-Mononobe clan conflict. Prince Shōtoku champions Buddhism and issues the Seventeen-Article Constitution (604). The Taika Reforms of 645 centralize government on the Chinese model — land nationalization, tax reform, provincial administration. Japan transforms from a clan confederation into a Chinese-style centralized state.
BuddhismTaika ReformsShōtoku
Loanwords
5th – 8th Century
Chinese Language — The Foundation of Written Japanese
The adoption of Chinese characters (kanji) and Sino-Japanese on'yomi readings constitutes the most massive linguistic borrowing in Japanese history. Thousands of Chinese-origin compounds form the backbone of Japanese vocabulary for governance, philosophy, religion, and science.

5th – 7th Century
Confucianism & Daoism Enter Japan
Arriving alongside Buddhism as ethical and cosmological frameworks from China. Confucian hierarchy — ruler-subject, parent-child — shaped governance and social order. Daoist concepts of yin-yang, five elements, and directional cosmology became embedded in onmyōdō court divination.

古典時代

Classical Japan

710 – 1185 CE
Historical Event
710 – 794 CE
Nara — Japan's First Permanent Capital
Modeled on Tang Chang'an, Nara becomes the seat of imperial power. The great Tōdai-ji temple and its colossal bronze Buddha symbolize state-Buddhist fusion. The Kojiki (712) and Nihon Shoki (720) codify mythology and imperial genealogy. Monks gain dangerous political influence, prompting the capital's move to Kyoto.
NaraTōdai-jiKojiki
Historical Event
794 – 1185 CE
Heian Period — The Golden Age of Court Culture
The capital moves to Heian-kyō (Kyoto). The Fujiwara clan dominates through regency. This era produces The Tale of Genji (world's first novel, by Murasaki Shikibu), The Pillow Book, and an exquisitely refined aristocratic culture. Kana syllabaries develop, enabling Japanese literary expression independent of Chinese. The court's elegance masks growing provincial instability.
KyotoFujiwaraGenjiKana
Religion
710 – 794 CE
Nara Six Schools (Nanto Rokushū)
Hossō (Yogācāra consciousness-only), Sanron (Mādhyamaka emptiness), Kegon (Huayan interdependence), Ritsu (monastic discipline), Kusha, and Jōjitsu — primarily scholastic traditions focused on philosophy over popular devotion, centered at the great Nara temples.
HossōKegonRitsu
Religion
816 CE
Shingon-shū — Kūkai's Esoteric Buddhism
Founded by Kūkai (Kōbō Daishi) on Mount Kōya. Esoteric (mikkyō) Buddhism centered on mantra, mandala, and mudra. All phenomena manifest the cosmic Buddha Dainichi Nyorai. Buddhahood achievable in this very body (sokushin jōbutsu). Kūkai also created the kana syllabary and founded Japan's first public school.
ShingonEsotericKūkai
Religion
805 CE
Tendai-shū — The Mother School
Founded by Saichō on Mount Hiei. Synthesized the Lotus Sutra, esoteric ritual, Zen meditation, and Pure Land chanting into a comprehensive path. Became the "mother school" from which virtually all later Kamakura-era sects — Pure Land, Zen, Nichiren — would emerge.
TendaiSaichōLotus Sutra
Religion
Heian Era
Shinbutsu-shūgō — Kami-Buddha Fusion
The dominant religious paradigm for a millennium: Shinto kami reinterpreted as manifestations of Buddhas and bodhisattvas (honji suijaku theory). Amaterasu identified with Dainichi Nyorai. Shrine-temples (jingū-ji) housed both traditions under one roof.
SyncretismHonji Suijaku
Foreign Contact
7th – 9th Century
Tang China & India (Indirect) — Esoteric Transmission
Kūkai and Saichō travel to Tang China and return with transformative teachings. Shingon's rituals trace to Indian Vajrayāna masters who transmitted to China. Sanskrit (Siddhaṃ script) became a sacred liturgical language still used in Shingon ritual. Japan suspends embassies to Tang China in 894 as it develops its own cultural identity.

Mythology
Heian Era
Mappō, Onryō & the Expanding Supernatural World
The world enters the "Latter Day of the Law" (mappō) — Buddhist practice degenerates and enlightenment becomes impossible. Vengeful spirits (onryō) terrorize the court: Sugawara no Michizane is deified as Tenjin after earthquakes follow his exile. Kitsune (foxes), tengu, and the hyakki yagyō (night parade of demons) populate Heian folklore.
MappōOnryōTenjin
Culture
Heian Era
Onmyōdō — Court Divination & Cosmic Order
Court-sanctioned system blending Chinese cosmology with native belief. Abe no Seimei, the legendary onmyōji, exemplified its prestige — casting horoscopes, banishing demons, and advising emperors. Directional taboos, lucky days, and ritual purification governed aristocratic life.
OnmyōdōAbe no Seimei
Historical Event
1180 – 1185 CE
Genpei War — Rise of the Warrior Class
The cataclysmic Genpei War between the Taira and Minamoto clans ends Heian court supremacy. The naval battle of Dan-no-ura (1185) — where the child emperor drowns with the imperial sword — becomes one of Japan's defining mythic-historical moments. Minamoto no Yoritomo founds the Kamakura shogunate, inaugurating seven centuries of warrior rule.
GenpeiSamuraiDan-no-ura
封建時代

Feudal Japan

1185 – 1603 CE
Historical Event
1185 – 1333 CE
Kamakura Shogunate — Japan's First Military Government
Minamoto no Yoritomo establishes the bakufu in Kamakura, creating a dual government with the imperial court in Kyoto. The Hōjō clan rules as regents after Yoritomo's death. Warrior values — loyalty, discipline, frugality — replace courtly elegance as cultural ideals.
BakufuSamurai
Historical Event
1274 & 1281 CE
Mongol Invasions — Divine Wind Saves Japan
Kublai Khan launches two massive invasions — the largest naval assaults in world history until D-Day. Japanese samurai resist on the beaches of Hakata Bay. Both fleets are devastated by typhoons — the "kamikaze" (divine wind). The invasions bankrupt the Kamakura government but cement Japan's belief in divine protection.
🇲🇳 Mongol Empire (Yuan Dynasty)
Mongol InvasionKamikaze
Religion
1175 – 1260s
Pure Land Revolution — Buddhism for Everyone
Hōnen founds Jōdo-shū (1175): salvation through exclusive nembutsu — "Namu Amida Butsu." His disciple Shinran radicalizes further with Jōdo Shinshū: even one sincere nembutsu suffices; monks may marry. This evolves into the powerful Hongan-ji institution and the militant Ikkō-ikki peasant-warrior leagues that control entire provinces.
Jōdo-shūJōdo ShinshūIkkō-ikki
Religion
1191 – 1253
Zen Buddhism — Rinzai & Sōtō
Eisai introduces Rinzai Zen from Song China (1191): enlightenment through kōan riddles and sudden insight, patronized by samurai. Dōgen founds Sōtō Zen (1227): shikantaza — objectless sitting — where practice itself is enlightenment. Zen reshapes Japanese aesthetics, martial culture, and the tea ceremony.
RinzaiSōtōKōan
Religion
1253
Nichiren-shū — The Lotus Sutra Alone
The prophet-monk Nichiren declares only the Lotus Sutra contains true dharma. Chanting "Namu Myōhō Renge Kyō" is the sole valid practice. Fiercely exclusivist, he condemns all other schools and predicts national disaster if Japan doesn't convert. Repeatedly exiled and nearly executed, his movement grows into one of Japan's most influential religious traditions.
NichirenLotus Sutra
Historical Event
1467 – 1477 CE
Ōnin War — Japan Shatters into Civil War
A succession dispute in the Ashikaga shogunate ignites the Ōnin War. Kyoto is devastated. The conflict triggers the Sengoku period — a century of constant warfare among hundreds of rival lords (daimyō). The old social order collapses; the age of the self-made warrior begins.
Ōnin WarSengoku
Foreign Contact
1543 – 1590s
Portugal — Guns, God, and the Namban Trade
Portuguese traders land on Tanegashima in 1543, introducing firearms (teppō) that revolutionize warfare. Francis Xavier arrives in 1549, beginning the Jesuit mission. Portuguese merchants establish trade through Nagasaki, importing guns, glass, tobacco, bread, and Christianity. The "Namban" (southern barbarian) trade transforms material culture.

1590s
Spain — Franciscans & the Manila Trade
Spanish Franciscans arrive from Manila competing with Jesuits. The San Felipe incident (1596) leads to the crucifixion of 26 Christians in Nagasaki — Japan's first martyrs. Spain intensifies missionary activity, fueling Hideyoshi's suspicion of foreign religion as a prelude to colonization.

Historical Event
1568 – 1603 CE
The Three Unifiers — Nobunaga, Hideyoshi, Ieyasu
Oda Nobunaga begins Japan's reunification, burning the Tendai fortress of Enryaku-ji (1571) and crushing the Ikkō-ikki. After his assassination (1582), Toyotomi Hideyoshi completes unification and launches invasions of Korea. Tokugawa Ieyasu wins the Battle of Sekigahara (1600) — the largest samurai battle ever — and founds the Tokugawa shogunate in 1603.
NobunagaHideyoshiSekigahara
Culture
14th – 16th Century
Zen Aesthetics — Wabi-Sabi, Tea & Noh
Zen Buddhism's aesthetic influence pervades Japanese culture: rock gardens (karesansui) distill nature to stone and sand; the tea ceremony (chadō) elevates mundane acts to spiritual practice; Noh theater sacralizes myth on stage; ink painting (suiboku-ga) captures essence in empty space. Wabi-sabi — beauty in imperfection and impermanence — becomes a defining Japanese sensibility.
Wabi-SabiTeaNoh
Mythology
Feudal Era
Warrior Mythos, Yōkai & the Sacred Stage
The samurai construct bushidō from Zen, Confucianism, and Shinto — a mythology of honor, loyalty, and beautiful death. Feudal folklore expands the supernatural bestiary: tengu, oni, kappa, tanuki. Zeami perfects Noh theater where ghosts, gods, and demons manifest. The kamikaze divine wind becomes central to Japan's identity as a sacred nation.
BushidōYōkaiNoh

Throughout Feudal Era
Shugendō — Mountain Asceticism
Syncretic path blending esoteric Buddhism, Shinto, and Daoism. Yamabushi mountain monks practiced extreme austerities — fire-walking, waterfall meditation, wilderness fasting — to gain supernatural powers (genjutsu). Neither purely Buddhist nor Shinto, Shugendō embodied Japan's spiritual syncretism.
ShugendōYamabushi

近世

Early Modern Japan

1603 – 1868 CE
Historical Event
1603 CE
Tokugawa Shogunate — 250 Years of Enforced Peace
Tokugawa Ieyasu establishes the Edo bakufu. The sankin-kōtai system forces daimyō to alternate residence between domains and Edo, ensuring loyalty and stimulating roads, commerce, and urbanization. Rigid class hierarchy (samurai, farmer, artisan, merchant) creates order. Edo (Tokyo) grows to over a million — the world's largest city by 1720.
EdoTokugawaSankin-kōtai
Historical Event
1637 – 1639 CE
Shimabara Rebellion & Sakoku — Japan Closes Its Doors
The Shimabara Rebellion — a desperate uprising of Christian peasants and rōnin — ends in the massacre of 37,000. Japan implements sakoku (closed country): Christianity is eradicated, virtually all foreigners are expelled, Japanese citizens are forbidden from leaving, and oceanic shipbuilding is banned. Only the Dutch (on Dejima) and Chinese retain limited trading rights.
ShimabaraSakokuIsolation
Foreign Contact
1641 – 1853
The Netherlands — Japan's Window to the West
The Dutch East India Company (VOC) maintains a trading post on Dejima island — the sole European contact during sakoku. "Dutch studies" (rangaku) gives Japanese scholars access to Western medicine, anatomy, astronomy, botany, and military science. The anatomical translation Kaitai Shinsho (1774) revolutionizes Japanese medicine.
🇳🇱 Netherlands (Dejima)

Religion
Edo Period
Danka Buddhism — Temple as State Tool
Every household must register at a Buddhist temple for surveillance and anti-Christian enforcement. While ensuring institutional power, this hollowed spiritual vitality. Funerary rites became Buddhism's primary social function — "funeral Buddhism" (sōshiki bukkyō). Buddhist clergy became bureaucrats rather than spiritual teachers.
DankaFuneral Buddhism
Religion
1630s – 1870s
Kakure Kirishitan — Hidden Christians
Driven underground by persecution, crypto-Christians maintained faith for over 200 years without priests, disguising icons as Buddhist statues and transmitting prayers (orashio) orally. When missionaries returned after 1853, they discovered communities still practicing — one of history's most remarkable acts of religious endurance.
Hidden ChristiansPersecution
Religion
18th Century
Kokugaku — National Learning Movement
Scholars like Motoori Norinaga seek to strip away Buddhist and Chinese "contamination" and recover pure ancient Shinto. They exalt the Kojiki, the emperor's divine nature, and Japan's unique spiritual identity. Kokugaku ideas would fuel the Meiji Restoration and construction of State Shinto.
KokugakuNorinaga
Culture
17th – 19th Century
Ukiyo-e, Kabuki & the Floating World
Edo culture flourishes: Hokusai's Great Wave and Hiroshige's landscapes transform world art; Kuniyoshi's warrior prints mythologize the past; kabuki theater electrifies audiences; Bashō perfects haiku; the pleasure quarters of Yoshiwara inspire literature, art, and music. Popular religion thrives through pilgrimage, Inari worship, and ghost story gatherings.
HokusaiKabukiHaiku
Mythology
Edo Period
Ghost Stories, Yōkai Catalogues & the Seven Lucky Gods
The Edo period codifies Japan's supernatural imagination. Yotsuya Kaidan and Ugetsu Monogatari weave Buddhist morality into tales of vengeful spirits. The hyakumonogatari kaidankai ("100 Supernatural Tales") becomes a parlor game. Artists catalogue yōkai systematically. The Shichi Fukujin (Seven Lucky Gods) — blending Shinto, Buddhist, Daoist, and Hindu deities — symbolize Japan's joyful syncretism.
KaidanYōkaiSeven Gods
Historical Event
1853 – 1868 CE
Perry's Black Ships & the Fall of the Shogunate
Commodore Matthew Perry arrives in Edo Bay with steam-powered warships (1853), shattering sakoku. The resulting "unequal treaties" with America, Britain, France, Russia, and the Netherlands humiliate Japan and trigger the sonnō jōi ("revere emperor, expel barbarians") crisis. The Tokugawa shogunate collapses. The Meiji Restoration of 1868 ends 265 years of warrior rule.

New Religion
1814 – 1838
Kurozumikyō & Tenrikyō — First New Religions
As Edo social order frays, charismatic founders launch new movements outside established temples. Kurozumikyō (1814): solar-worship Shinto revelation. Tenrikyō (1838): Nakayama Miki's revelatory experience of God the Parent (Tenri-Ō-no-Mikoto) promising the "joyous life" for all. These precursors foreshadow the explosion of new religions in the modern era.

現代日本

Modern Japan

1868 CE – Present
Historical Event
1868 – 1912 CE
Meiji Restoration — From Feudalism to World Power
Imperial rule restored. Japan modernizes at astonishing speed: railways, telegraph, conscript army, public education, Meiji Constitution (1889). Victory in the Sino-Japanese War (1895) gains Taiwan; victory over Russia (1905) — the first modern Asian defeat of a European power — shocks the world and inspires anti-colonial movements across Asia. Japan annexes Korea in 1910.
MeijiModernizationRusso-Japanese War
Religion
1868 – 1945
State Shinto — Religion as National Ideology
The Meiji government decrees shinbutsu bunri — forced separation of Shinto and Buddhism after a millennium of fusion. The haibutsu kishaku ("abolish Buddhism") movement destroys thousands of temples. State Shinto is constructed centering on emperor worship, divine origins, and Yasukuni Shrine. Officially "not a religion" but mandatory civic observance.
State ShintoHaibutsu Kishaku

1868 – 1912
Western Powers — Wholesale Modernization
The Iwakura Mission (1871–73) studies Western nations. Japan adopts Prussian military structure and constitution, British naval tradition, French civil law, American agricultural methods, and German medical science. Thousands of foreign advisors (oyatoi gaikokujin) are hired. Christianity is re-legalized in 1873.

Historical Event
1941 – 1945 CE
World War II — Imperial Expansion and Atomic Devastation
Japan attacks Pearl Harbor (1941) and conquers much of Asia and the Pacific. State Shinto mythology reaches its zenith — the emperor is a living god (arahitogami), kamikaze pilots invoke divine wind. The war kills millions across Asia. Atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki (August 1945) end the war and reshape the global order. Japan surrenders unconditionally on August 15, 1945.

Historical Event
1945 – 1970s CE
Postwar Rebirth — From Ashes to Economic Miracle
Emperor Hirohito's Humanity Declaration (1946) renounces divine status. The new constitution guarantees religious freedom, women's suffrage, and the renunciation of war (Article 9). American occupation reshapes institutions. Japan's "economic miracle" transforms the devastated nation into the world's second-largest economy by the 1960s, with innovations in electronics, automobiles, and manufacturing.

The American occupation (1945–52) under MacArthur reshapes Japan's constitution, education, land reform, and popular culture. American jazz, film, fashion, democratic ideals, and consumer culture flood in. English becomes the dominant foreign language. The US-Japan security alliance anchors Cold War strategy. American cultural influence remains pervasive — from baseball to fast food to Hollywood.

Ōmoto-kyō — The Mother of New Religions
Founded by Deguchi Nao. Shinto-derived millenarian movement prophesying world renewal. Profoundly influential — Ōmoto spawned or inspired Seicho-no-Ie, Worldmate, and others. Twice violently suppressed by the imperial state (1921, 1935) for challenging the emperor's spiritual monopoly.

Nichiren Buddhist lay organization. Founder Makiguchi died in prison for refusing State Shinto compliance. Under Toda and Ikeda, it grew explosively through aggressive proselytization (shakubuku) to millions of members. Founded the Kōmeitō political party. Cult

Aum Shinrikyō — Apocalyptic Terror
Founded by Asahara Shōkō. An apocalyptic cult blending Vajrayāna Buddhism, Hindu deities (Shiva), Christian eschatology, and science fiction. Committed the 1995 Tokyo subway sarin gas attack, killing 13 and injuring thousands. The attack traumatized Japan and stigmatized all new religious movements. Asahara executed in 2018. Remnants reorganized as Aleph and Hikari no Wa.

Blends self-help, new age spirituality, and political conservatism. Ōkawa died in 2023; the movement's future is uncertain.

Tōhoku Earthquake, Tsunami & Fukushima
A magnitude 9.0 earthquake — the most powerful in Japan's recorded history — triggers a devastating tsunami that kills nearly 20,000. The Fukushima Daiichi nuclear meltdown becomes the worst nuclear disaster since Chernobyl. The triple catastrophe reshapes Japan's energy policy, emergency preparedness, and national psyche.

Mythology
1868 – Present
From State Myth to Anime — Mythology's Modern Life
Meiji government repackaged ancient myths as literal national history. Postwar, the emperor became human and myths became "just stories." Yet mythology found new life: Miyazaki's films reimagine Shinto animism; Amaterasu appears in Ōkami; yōkai fill Pokémon; Susanoo and Tsukuyomi populate Naruto. Matsuri festivals keep mythology alive in embodied practice across thousands of communities.
AnimeMiyazakiMatsuri

1980s – Present
Global Soft Power — Japan's Cultural Export
Japanese culture flows outward: anime, manga, J-pop, video games (Nintendo, Sony), cuisine (sushi, ramen), fashion (Comme des Garçons, kawaii), and design influence the world. "Cool Japan" becomes a government strategy. Japan transitions from cultural importer to one of the world's most influential cultural exporters, while maintaining deep traditional roots.
